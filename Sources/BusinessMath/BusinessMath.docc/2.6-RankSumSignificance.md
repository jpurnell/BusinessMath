# Rank-Sum Significance with Ties

Exact critical values for whether one item's rank sum stands out from a panel of judges, with tied rankings handled exactly.

## Overview

A tasting panel of `m` judges each ranks the same `n` wines. A wine's **rank sum** is the
total of the ranks it received. Kramer's rank-sum test asks whether a sum is too low or too
high to have arisen from judges with no shared preference. BusinessMath answers that from the
**exact** null distribution, ties included:

- ``rankSumDistribution(judgeRanks:)`` returns the distribution of one wine's rank sum,
  ``RankSumDistribution``.
- ``rankSumCriticalValues(judgeRanks:alpha:method:)`` returns the two-sided critical values,
  ``RankSumCriticalValues``, at level α.

Both take the same input as ``concordanceAnalysis(_:)``: one row per judge, each a full set of
that judge's ranks for all `n` wines, with tied wines given the **average** of the places they
span.

```swift
let ranks: [[Double]] = [
    [1, 2,   3,   4, 5],
    [1, 2.5, 2.5, 4, 5],   // this judge could not separate wines 2 and 3
    [2, 1,   3,   5, 4],
    [1, 3,   2,   4, 5],
]
let values = try rankSumCriticalValues(judgeRanks: ranks, alpha: 0.05)
values.lower                 // 6: a rank sum of 6 or less is significantly low
values.upper                 // 18
values.nonSignificantRange   // 6.5...17.5
values.isSignificant(6)      // true
```

## The method

Under the null hypothesis each judge's ranking is a uniformly random arrangement of that
judge's own ranks. Whatever the judge did with ties, a given wine's rank from that judge is
then **uniform over the judge's multiset of ranks**, and independent across judges. The rank
sum is a sum of `m` independent discrete uniforms, and its distribution is their convolution.

Averaged ranks are multiples of ½, so the convolution runs over **doubled** sums, where every
rank is an integer. It counts outcomes rather than adding probabilities: each sum's
probability is an exact integer count of the `n^m` equally likely outcomes, held in
multi-limb integers because `n^m` overflows every fixed-width type within a dozen judges. Each
judge's ranks split into runs (a stretch of untied places, or one tied group), and convolving
with a run is a sliding sum, so an untied judge costs one pass over the support.

The mean is `m(n + 1)/2`. The variance is `Σⱼ [(n² − 1)/12 − Σ(t³ − t)/(12n)]`, where `t` runs
over the sizes of judge `j`'s tied groups. Without ties that is `m(n² − 1)/12`.

### What ties change

A judge who ties wines contributes a narrower uniform than one who does not, so a tied panel
has a smaller variance and **tighter** critical values than the untied table for the same `m`
and `n`. Using untied breakpoints on a tied tasting makes the test conservative on average,
and wrong in the particular: the untied bounds are simply not the critical values of that
panel. A judge who ties every wine gives every wine `(n + 1)/2` and only shifts the
distribution. With ties the distribution need not be symmetric, so the two bounds need not be
equidistant from the mean, and one can exist without the other.

## The convention

At two-sided level α:

- ``RankSumCriticalValues/lower`` is the **largest attainable** sum `L` with `P(S ≤ L) ≤ α/2`;
- ``RankSumCriticalValues/upper`` is the **smallest attainable** sum `U` with `P(S ≥ U) ≤ α/2`;
- a wine whose sum is `≤ L` or `≥ U` is significant. The bounds are themselves significant.

Published tables differ in what they print. ``RankSumCriticalValues/nonSignificantRange``
gives the other reading, the attainable sums strictly between `L` and `U`: `L + 1 … U − 1`
without ties, and in half-steps with them. ``RankSumCriticalValues/lowerTailProbability`` and
``RankSumCriticalValues/upperTailProbability`` are the tail probabilities the bounds actually
achieve, which for a discrete distribution are usually below α/2.

### When there are none

The bounds are `nil` when no attainable sum is extreme enough:

- **Too small a panel.** Two judges and five wines: the lowest possible sum has probability
  1/25 = 0.04 > 0.025, so nothing is significant at 5%.
  ``RankSumCriticalValues/canReachSignificance`` is `false`.
- **A panel that tied everything.** Every wine's sum is `m(n + 1)/2` with certainty;
  ``RankSumCriticalValues/isDegenerate`` is `true`, nothing is significant by either method,
  and the non-significant range is that one sum. This is reported, not thrown: "no wine
  differs" is the true answer, and a caller should say there are no breakpoints rather than
  show numbers.

## The exactness guarantee

For the exact method, every comparison of a tail with α/2 is made in exact integer
arithmetic: the tail as a count of outcomes, and α as the exact binary value of the `alpha`
passed in. The bounds are the true critical values of the exact null distribution at that
level for every panel the method accepts, however close a tail lies to α/2. Only reported
probabilities are rounded, once each.

That is not a nicety. Two judges and twenty wines give `P(S ≤ 5) = 10/400`, exactly 0.025.
Summed in `Double` it comes out above the rounded 0.025, and a floating-point comparison
puts the lower bound at 4 instead of 5.

### Size

Building the distribution costs about `2m²n · width · runs` limb operations, where
`width ≈ m·log₂ n / 64` limbs and `runs` is the number of untied stretches and tie groups
per judge (one for an untied judge). Memory is two tables of `2m(n − 1) × width` limbs. So
time grows as roughly the cube of the number of judges, and linearly in the number of wines.
Measured in a release build on Apple silicon (arm64), called from another module as an app
would. The machine was shared, so treat the figures as ±30%. "Light ties" means every other
judge has two tied pairs and a three-way tie; "scores 1–5" means 1–5 point scores, ranked,
which gives about five tie groups per judge:

| Panel (judges × wines) | Untied | Light ties | Scores 1–5 | Peak memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 × 8 | 0.06 ms | 0.06 ms | — | < 10 MB |
| 50 × 20 | 1.6 ms | 12 ms | — | < 10 MB |
| 100 × 50 | 0.025 s | 0.10 s | 0.14 s | 5 MB |
| 100 × 100 | 0.055 s | 0.23 s | 0.33 s | 18 MB |
| 200 × 50 | 0.21 s | 0.9 s | 1.3 s | 16 MB |
| 300 × 50 | 0.77 s | 3.6 s | 5.3 s | 17 MB |
| 300 × 100 | 2.1 s | 10 s | 18 s | 36 MB |
| 500 × 50 | 5.9 s | 23 s | 20 s | 41 MB |
| 1000 × 50 | 44 s | — | — | 152 MB |

Once a distribution is built, ``RankSumDistribution/criticalValues(alpha:)`` takes about a
millisecond, so build it once per panel and reuse it for every level.

The method stays exact at every size here; the limit is time. For an interactive app the
exact method is comfortable up to about 200 judges × 100 wines (about a second with ties).
For larger panels, compute it off the main thread or use the normal approximation. A panel
whose table would exceed 2²⁶ limbs (512 MiB; 3,000 judges × 100 wines, for example) is refused with
`BusinessMathError.resourceExhausted` before anything is allocated.

## The normal approximation

``RankSumCriticalValueMethod/normalApproximation`` gives `mean ∓ z·σ`, with `z` the
`1 − α/2` normal quantile and σ the exact, tie-corrected standard deviation. It is an
**approximation**: the thresholds fall between attainable sums and are not rounded to any, so
its nominal tails of α/2 are not what the panel achieves, and it has no non-significant range.
It does not build the distribution, so it costs nothing at any size. Use it for comparison,
or for panels too large to count.

## Testing every wine

These critical values test **one** wine. Testing every wine in a tasting at α makes it far
more likely than α that *some* wine is flagged when none differs. On a 24-judge, 12-wine null
panel with ties, about 44% of panels have at least one wine outside its 5% bounds (measured by
permutation in the package's reference tests). BusinessMath does not adjust for this; a
caller that reports every significant wine should say that the level applies per wine.

## Kramer (1963)

Kramer's revised tables (Food Technology 17(12), 1596–1597) are the usual published source
for untied panels. They do **not** agree with the exact per-wine test. Comparing every cell
of the 5% table, as WineTaster transcribed it, against the exact untied critical values:

- read as the non-significant range, 54 of 165 cells equal the exact 5% two-sided values, and
  82 the exact 10% values; read as the significant bounds, 13 and 43;
- ten cells are not symmetric about the mean (`low + high ≠ m(n + 1)`), so they are
  transcription slips in the copy or in the source;
- in most of the rest the table's non-significant range is one or two sums narrower on each
  side than the exact 5% range, so the table flags more wines than a 5% test should.

The package's reference tests pin these counts.

## Validation

- **Brute force:** every combination of per-judge positions, and separately every permutation
  of every judge's row, for panels up to 5 wines × 4 judges, with and without ties.
- **Exact integer arithmetic:** Python's arbitrary-precision integers, for small panels and for
  large ones (100 × 50 tied and untied, 200 × 20, 150 × 30, 60 × 40): pmfs, tails and
  critical values at α = 0.05, 0.10 and 0.01, every one matched exactly.
- **R** (base R, a second convolution and critical-value rule in another language).
- **Monte Carlo** by permuting whole rows: 400 000 draws on a 24 × 12 tied panel.

The generator is `Scripts/reference-fixtures/generate_rank_sum.py`.
